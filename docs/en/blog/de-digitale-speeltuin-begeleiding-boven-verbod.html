The Digital Playground: Guidance Over Bans

The Digital Playground: Why a Ban Doesn't Solve the Problem

In a recent opinion piece in the Volkskrant, Kim Schuurman argues that a social media ban for young people resembles “adult panic politics” more than effective policy. She advocates for a “digital playground” instead of a “digital fence,” rightly highlighting young people’s rights: the right to access, participate, and develop. It’s a strong and necessary stance. Yet, the comparison to a physical playground isn’t entirely straightforward. That very nuance is essential if we truly want to support parents and young people in raising children safely in the digital age. (Read the original article: https://www.volkskrant.nl/columns-opinie/opinie-een-socialemediaverbod-voor-jongeren-is-volwassen-paniekpolitiek~bf9275a9/ )

The Playground as a Metaphor: A Strong, Yet Nuanced Comparison

A physical playground is not a free-for-all for everyone. Stairs leading to a playhouse, swings, or climbing frames are designed with age limits in mind. Younger children simply can’t reach or climb certain structures, which automatically protects them from risks that don’t suit their stage of development. The digital world lacks this natural design. Algorithms, infinite scroll feeds, and unrestricted access to content are often optimized for adult users, not for children’s development. A well-designed digital environment should therefore follow that same physical principle: gradually building accessibility, with clear boundaries that align with the child’s developmental stage.

Safety Starts with Design, Not Bans

Schuurman is right that protection shouldn’t lead to exclusion. Yet, responsibility shouldn’t rest solely on parents or children. Just as with physical toys and play spaces, digital platforms and apps must meet safety and design standards. Fortunately, this is changing: from mid-2025, the EU will enforce minimum requirements for the digital safety of smart devices, including digital toys. This is a step in the right direction. However, for social media, design is still often geared toward maximizing engagement rather than child-friendly development. Platforms must be required to implement “age-appropriate design”: no manipulative notifications, no infinite feeds, and clear parental control options set up as helpful tools, not obstacles.

The Parent’s Role: From Passive Observer to Active Guide

The biggest nuance in the playground metaphor, however, lies in the parent’s role. At a physical playground, a parent or older child helps a younger one climb the steps, stays nearby, and keeps an eye on the situation. Digitally, it’s often different. A smartphone or tablet is frequently handed over like a “swimming mask” with the message: “Good luck!” Meanwhile, the Bureau for Youth & Media warns that we’re pushing children to the edge of a digital pool without supervision. True digital literacy doesn’t come from rules alone, but from active involvement. Clear agreements—no screens at the table, no phones in the bedroom—only work when they’re explained and established together. Parents must make use of that pedagogical space: offering support, guidance, and trust. Not fear-mongering, but a secure foundation where children can ask questions about what they encounter online.

Working Together: Agreements, Guidance, and Children’s Rights

Children have the right to participate, access information, and enjoy a safe online environment. An outright ban ignores these rights and actually pushes young people toward unsupervised, unregulated apps. At the same time, the right to access doesn’t mean the right to unlimited access. The solution lies in the middle ground: a digital environment designed with children’s development in mind, guided by involved adults, and shaped alongside young people themselves. As Schuurman rightly points out, young people must be systematically included in policy-making. Parents can lead by example by co-creating a digital agreement with their child, testing apps together, and regularly reflecting on online experiences. This builds resilience, not fear.

Conclusion & Next Steps

The comparison to a physical playground is powerful, but it requires nuance. We don’t have to choose between a ban or a free-for-all. We must choose a securely designed digital world, guided by engaged parents, where young people gradually learn to navigate. Start today with an honest conversation about what your child is experiencing online. Monimentor supports families by establishing gentle boundaries, helping children learn self-regulation while giving you as a parent the control and insight needed to foster these conversations. We’re also part of a broader digital ecosystem, where significant progress can be made through better digital environment design. Together, we can make the digital playground both safe and playful.
